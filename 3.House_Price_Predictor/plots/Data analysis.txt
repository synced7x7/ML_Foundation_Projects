Image 1 — EDA overview
The target distribution is the most important panel here. I can see the classic shape: a roughly bell-ish distribution from 0 to ~4.5, then a massive spike at exactly 5.0 containing about 2,000 points. That spike is the data cap — every house "worth" $500k or more got recorded as exactly 5.0. The log-transform in the next panel compresses the right side nicely and makes the bulk distribution look more symmetric, but notice the spike is still there at log(6)≈1.79 — log transforms don't fix capping, they just change its location on the axis.
The MedInc vs price scatter shows a strong, clean positive relationship (corr=0.87) — and I can see the cap's fingerprint here too: there's a horizontal band of points sitting exactly at y=5.0 across the entire income range, including low-income points. That's data integrity damage — some low-income areas are forced to look like high-income ones in the target variable purely because of the cap.
The geographic map confirms the synthetic-data issue I mentioned — it's just colored noise with no structure. On the real dataset this plot would show a clear green cluster around San Francisco and LA.
The correlation matrix and the feature-correlation bar chart reveal something important for next steps: income_x_age has almost the same correlation with the target as MedInc itself (~0.7 vs 0.87), and the two are themselves correlated at ~0.7. This is multicollinearity — two features carrying almost the same information. Also notice bedrms_per_room is the single most negatively correlated feature (-0.24), more than any raw original feature except AveOccup. That's a genuinely useful engineered feature.
Image 2 — Model evaluation
The Actual vs Predicted plot (R²=0.790) shows a tight, well-aligned cloud along the diagonal for actual values between about 1 and 4 — that's the model working well in its "normal" range. But look at x=5: there's a dense vertical column of points where actual=5 but predicted ranges anywhere from ~1 to ~7. The model has no way to know these are special capped values, so it predicts what the features suggest the price should be — sometimes correctly high, sometimes way off. This single column is responsible for a large chunk of error.
The residual plot has an unusual feature: a clean diagonal streak running from (predicted≈1, residual≈+1.5) down to (predicted≈7, residual≈-2). This isn't random — it's mathematically forced. For every capped point, residual = 5 − predicted. As predicted increases linearly, residual decreases linearly at slope -1. That perfect diagonal line is the set of capped points, plotted as a deterministic function of the model's prediction. Outside that streak, the rest of the cloud looks reasonably random around zero, which is the good sign I want to see.
The residual distribution has skew = -0.296, meaning a longer tail toward negative residuals (overprediction). Combined with the diagonal streak above (which produces large negative residuals at high predicted values), this tells the model occasionally predicts a high price for a house that's actually cheap — likely for cheap houses that happen to share features with expensive capped ones.
The Q-Q plot tracks the diagonal closely in the middle (R²=0.9947 for the line fit) but bends away at both tails — the classic signature of a distribution with heavier tails than normal, again traceable to the cap.
The feature importance panel has the most surprising and important finding: income_x_age has a negative coefficient, despite being positively correlated with the target on its own (per Image 1's correlation chart). This is the textbook symptom of multicollinearity — when two features (MedInc and income_x_age) carry overlapping information, linear regression can assign one of them a sign-flipped coefficient that "corrects" for double-counting by the other. The coefficient itself becomes uninterpretable in isolation. MedInc absorbs almost all the explanatory power (coefficient ~1.15, dwarfing everything else), and income_x_age's coefficient is really just a correction term, not a meaningful "age matters more for rich areas" signal. If I wanted that interaction to be interpretable, I'd need to drop MedInc or income_x_age, or use Ridge with a higher alpha to shrink the correlated pair together.
The model comparison panel shows baseline at R²=-0.041, which surprises a lot of people — shouldn't a constant prediction give R²=0? It gives exactly 0 only if predict the test set's mean. I predicted the training set's median, which is a slightly different number, so the model performs marginally worse than "perfect constant guessing" on this particular test set — hence the small negative value. It's not a bug, just a reminder that R²=0 is defined relative to the test set's own mean, not any arbitrary constant. The jump from 0.790 (linear/Ridge) to 0.854 (Poly degree=2 + Ridge) is solid — the interaction and squared terms are capturing real non-linear structure.
Image 3 — Residual deep dive
The scale-location plot is the most visually striking chart here, and it needs careful reading. At first glance it looks like two separate curves: one rising gently from (predicted=1, √|resid|≈0.5) up to (predicted=4, √|resid|≈1.2), then a sharp V-shaped dip down to nearly 0 right at predicted=5, followed by a steep climb back up to ~1.5 by predicted=7. That V-shape at predicted=5 is, again, the capped points — by definition, points where the model happens to predict close to 5 have residual close to 0 (since actual=5 too), so √|residual| collapses to zero exactly at predicted=5 regardless of the broader trend. The trend line (mild positive slope) is trying to summarize all of this with a single straight line, which is a poor fit given the V-shape — this plot is telling the data has a structural break, not smooth heteroscedasticity, and a single regularized linear model can't represent both regimes well.
The residuals vs MedInc plot is genuinely the most informative diagnostic in the whole set. For low-to-mid MedInc (0–8), residuals are scattered fairly symmetrically in a wide band from about -1.5 to +1.5 — normal-looking noise. But above MedInc≈10, the residuals collapse into a tight, almost entirely negative band around -1.5 to -2. This means: for the highest-income areas, the model is consistently and substantially overpredicting actual price — wait, let's check the sign convention (residual = actual − predicted, so negative residual means predicted > actual, i.e., overprediction). Combined with what we saw in Image 2, this is the cap effect again: very high-income areas often have actual=5 (capped), but the model — extrapolating from the strong MedInc relationship — predicts something even higher than 5 for these areas, producing large negative residuals. This is the model "wanting" to predict $700k+ houses but the data ceiling preventing it from ever being right at the top end.


Problems that need to be adressed:
Three concrete next steps fall directly out of these plots. First, the $500k cap is the single biggest source of error — filtering out capped rows (or modeling them separately as a classification problem: "is this a capped luxury home, yes/no") would likely give the cleanest R² jump. Second, dropping either MedInc or income_x_age before next run — they're fighting each other for the same signal and making coefficients uninterpretable.
