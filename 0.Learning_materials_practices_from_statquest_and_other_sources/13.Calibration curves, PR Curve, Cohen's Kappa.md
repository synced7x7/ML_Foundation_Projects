
# 1. Calibration Curves — Are Your Predicted Probabilities Reliable?

Many machine learning models don't just predict a class.

Instead they predict a **probability**.

Example:

```text
Patient A

Cancer probability = 0.95
```

Another patient

```text
Patient B

Cancer probability = 0.60
```

The model says

> Patient A is much more likely to have cancer.

But here's the important question:

**Can we trust these probabilities?**

---

## What does "well calibrated" mean?

Suppose your model predicts

```text
80 patients

Probability = 0.80
```

If the model is well calibrated,

around

```text
64 patients (80%)
```

should actually have the disease.

If only

```text
20 patients
```

have it,

then

the model is **overconfident**.

---

Similarly,

Suppose the model predicts

```text
Probability = 0.30
```

for 100 people.

A calibrated model expects

```text
Around 30 people
```

to actually belong to that class.

---

## Calibration Curve

A calibration curve compares

```text
Predicted Probability

vs

Actual Frequency
```

---

Perfect calibration

| Predicted Probability | Actual Positive Rate |
| --------------------: | -------------------: |
|                   0.1 |                  0.1 |
|                   0.3 |                  0.3 |
|                   0.5 |                  0.5 |
|                   0.8 |                  0.8 |
|                   0.9 |                  0.9 |

The graph is a straight diagonal line.

```text
Actual

1.0 |               /
    |             /
0.8 |           /
    |         /
0.6 |       /
    |     /
0.4 |   /
    | /
0.2 |/
    +-------------------
      Predicted
```

This means

```text
Confidence = Reality
```

---

## Overconfident Model

Suppose

Model predicts

```text
90%
```

But only

```text
60%
```

are actually positive.

Graph

```text
Actual

1.0 |
0.8 |
0.6 |         *
0.4 |
0.2 |
    +--------------
       0.9
```

The curve lies **below** the diagonal.

Meaning

> The model is more confident than it should be.

---

## Underconfident Model

Predicts

```text
40%
```

but actually

```text
70%
```

are positive.

Now the curve lies **above** the diagonal.

Meaning

The model is **too cautious**.

---

## Why is calibration important?

Imagine a medical AI.

Patient A

```text
99% chance
```

Doctor immediately starts treatment.

But if

99%

really means

60%,

the model is dangerous.

Probability estimates matter in:

* Medical diagnosis
* Weather forecasting
* Loan approval
* Insurance
* Autonomous driving

---

## sklearn example

```python
from sklearn.calibration import CalibrationDisplay

CalibrationDisplay.from_estimator(model, X_test, y_test)
```

---

## Interview insight

Calibration does **not** measure accuracy.

It measures

> **Whether predicted probabilities match real-world probabilities.**

# 2. Precision-Recall Curve

Earlier we learned

ROC Curve

plots

```text
TPR

vs

FPR
```

This works well when

classes are balanced.

---

## Problem with ROC

Suppose

```text
10000 emails

9950 Normal

50 Spam
```

Extremely imbalanced.

Suppose the classifier

correctly detects

40 spam emails.

False positives

```text
20
```

False positive rate

[
FPR=\frac{20}{9950}
=0.002
]

Very tiny.

ROC looks excellent.

---

But precision

[
Precision=\frac{40}{40+20}
=67%
]

Meaning

One out of every three emails marked as spam is actually normal.

That's much more informative.

---

## Precision-Recall Curve

Instead of plotting

```text
TPR

vs

FPR
```

it plots

```text
Recall

vs

Precision
```

---

Example

Threshold changes

| Threshold | Precision | Recall |
| --------- | --------- | ------ |
| 0.9       | 100%      | 20%    |
| 0.7       | 90%       | 50%    |
| 0.5       | 75%       | 75%    |
| 0.3       | 60%       | 90%    |

As threshold decreases

Recall increases

Precision usually decreases.

---

Graph

```text
Precision

1.0 |\
    | \
0.8 |  \
    |    \
0.6 |      \
    |        \
0.4 +-------------
      Recall
```

Better curves stay near the top-right.

---

## Why is PR Curve better?

Imagine

Cancer detection

```text
Healthy = 9900

Cancer =100
```

Accuracy

99%

means nothing.

ROC may also look excellent because the huge number of healthy patients keeps the false positive rate very low.

PR Curve focuses **only on the positive (minority) class**, making it much more informative.

---

## Example

Model A

```text
ROC AUC = 0.98
```

Looks amazing.

But

```text
Precision = 30%

Recall = 95%
```

Meaning

70% of positive predictions are false alarms.

The PR Curve exposes this trade-off, whereas the ROC curve may still appear excellent.

---

## sklearn

```python
from sklearn.metrics import PrecisionRecallDisplay

PrecisionRecallDisplay.from_estimator(
    model,
    X_test,
    y_test
)
```

---

## Interview insight

Use

**ROC**

when

```text
Classes are balanced.
```

Use

**Precision-Recall**

when

```text
Positive class is rare.
```

Examples

* Fraud detection
* Cancer detection
* Credit card fraud
* Intrusion detection
* Manufacturing defects

# 3. Cohen's Kappa

Accuracy asks

> How often is the model correct?

Cohen's Kappa asks

> **How much better is the model than random guessing?**

---

## Why do we need it?

Imagine

95% students pass

5% fail.

Suppose your model predicts

```text
Pass

Pass

Pass

Pass
```

Always.

Accuracy

95%

Looks fantastic.

But

Even random guessing while heavily favoring the majority class would already achieve high accuracy.

Kappa corrects for this.

---

## Formula

[
\kappa=
\frac{P_o-P_e}
{1-P_e}
]

Where

* (P_o) = Observed agreement (accuracy)
* (P_e) = Expected agreement by chance

---

## Example

Suppose

Accuracy

```text
90%
```

Random agreement

```text
80%
```

Then

[
\kappa=
\frac{0.90-0.80}
{1-0.80}
========

0.50
]

Although accuracy is 90%,

Kappa is only

```text
0.50
```

Meaning

The model isn't as impressive as the accuracy suggests.

---

## Interpretation

| Kappa     | Meaning                  |
| --------- | ------------------------ |
| <0        | Worse than random        |
| 0         | Random agreement         |
| 0.01–0.20 | Slight agreement         |
| 0.21–0.40 | Fair agreement           |
| 0.41–0.60 | Moderate agreement       |
| 0.61–0.80 | Substantial agreement    |
| 0.81–1.00 | Almost perfect agreement |

---

## Example

Dataset

```text
100 patients

95 Healthy

5 Sick
```

Model predicts

Everyone Healthy.

Accuracy

```text
95%
```

Looks excellent.

Kappa

```text
≈0
```

Because the model is doing little better than always choosing the majority class.

---

## Another example

Model

```text
Accuracy = 92%

Kappa = 0.88
```

Now

Most of the agreement is **genuine**, not due to chance.

This is a much stronger model.

---

## sklearn

```python
from sklearn.metrics import cohen_kappa_score

kappa = cohen_kappa_score(y_true, y_pred)

print(kappa)
```

---

## Where is Cohen's Kappa used?

It is widely used to measure **agreement** between two raters or systems.

Examples:

* Two doctors diagnosing X-rays
* Two teachers grading essays
* Human labels vs AI predictions
* Human labels vs human labels

Instead of asking

> "How often do they agree?"

Kappa asks

> "How often do they agree **beyond what we'd expect by chance?**"

---

# Summary Table

| Metric                     | What it Measures                                           | Best Use Case                                                      | Why It's Important                                                                           |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Calibration Curve**      | Whether predicted probabilities match actual probabilities | Medical diagnosis, risk prediction, weather forecasting            | Ensures that a prediction like **80%** truly means about **80%** of such cases are positive  |
| **Precision-Recall Curve** | Trade-off between precision and recall across thresholds   | Imbalanced datasets (fraud, cancer, anomaly detection)             | Focuses on the minority class and is often more informative than ROC when positives are rare |
| **Cohen's Kappa**          | Agreement beyond what would occur by chance                | Imbalanced classification, human-vs-human or human-vs-AI agreement | Avoids being misled by high accuracy that comes simply from predicting the majority class    |

## Interview takeaway

If asked **"When would you use each?"**, a strong answer is:

* **Calibration Curve:** "When the predicted probabilities themselves are important—for example, estimating a patient's disease risk or a customer's default probability."
* **Precision-Recall Curve:** "When the positive class is rare and I care more about correctly identifying positives than about overall accuracy."
* **Cohen's Kappa:** "When I want to know whether my model's agreement is genuinely meaningful rather than just a consequence of class imbalance or chance agreement."
